Why Does My Transparent Logo Show a White Background in Outlook Email Signatures?

A transparent logo shows a white background in an Outlook email signature because Outlook — especially in Dark Mode — adds its own solid fill behind transparent PNGs instead of preserving the alpha channel. The logo file itself is usually fine; Outlook's rendering engine is forcing the white box. You can fix it by placing the image inside an HTML table cell, using a logo with a colored or outlined mark that reads on any background, or confirming the file is a true transparent PNG (not a JPEG that baked in white).

Why Outlook Adds a White Background to Transparent Logos

  • Outlook doesn't render PNG alpha transparency the way browsers do — its rendering engine (based on Word, on Windows) often substitutes a solid fill.
  • In Dark Mode, Outlook deliberately places a white or light box behind transparent images so they stay visible against a dark background — which looks like a bug but is intentional.
  • If the "transparent" logo was ever saved or exported as a JPEG, transparency was stripped and a real white background was baked in — that white is now part of the image.
  • The same signature can look perfect while composing but show the white box after sending, because the sender and recipient clients render the image differently.

How to Fix the White Background

  1. Confirm the file is truly transparent: Drag the logo into a browser tab — if it's see-through there but white in Outlook, the file is fine and the issue is Outlook's rendering.
  2. Wrap the logo in a table cell: In your signature HTML, place the <img> inside a <table><tr><td> structure — this helps Outlook preserve transparency in Dark Mode.
  3. Edit the signature file directly (Windows): Signatures live in C:\Users\<you>\AppData\Roaming\Microsoft\Signatures\ as .htm files — open and adjust the image markup there.
  4. Use a Dark-Mode-safe logo: Choose a version with a colored or outlined mark (or a subtle glow/stroke) so it reads cleanly on both white and dark backgrounds.
  5. Re-export as a real transparent PNG: If the white is baked into the file, remove the background again and export a true transparent PNG — never a JPEG.

Prepare Your Logo So It Never Boxes Out

  • Always use PNG (or SVG where supported) for signature logos — JPEG cannot hold transparency and will always show a solid background.
  • Keep a version of the logo with adequate contrast on dark backgrounds, since many recipients read email in Dark Mode.
  • Test your signature by sending it to yourself and viewing it in Outlook desktop, Outlook web, and the mobile app — rendering differs across all three.
  • Start from a clean, genuinely transparent cutout so the only variable left is Outlook's rendering, not the file.

Why dark mode is where this shows up

A transparent logo designed for a white background is usually dark-coloured artwork with nothing behind it. On a white email, that is invisible as a problem. Switch the reading pane to dark, and two bad things can happen at once: either the dark artwork sits on a dark background and becomes unreadable, or the client puts a light panel behind it so it stays visible — and that panel is the white box you are seeing.

Read that way, the white box is the client trying to help. It is a legibility fallback, not a bug, which is also why there is no setting to turn it off from your end.

The professional answer: don't depend on transparency here

This is the part worth being blunt about. Email signatures are the one place where transparency is a bad foundation, because you cannot control the background it will land on. The approaches that actually hold up:

  • A logo that reads on both light and dark. Dark artwork with a light outline, or a mark with enough internal contrast that it survives either background. This is a design decision, not a file-format one.
  • A logo with its own solid background, matching your brand — a coloured tile rather than a transparent cutout. If a box is going to appear anyway, make it a box you chose.
  • Keep it small and simple. Signature logos are rendered at modest sizes; fine detail and soft shadows are where edge artefacts show most.

Designers resist this because a transparent PNG feels more "correct". In a context where a third-party client decides your background, the reliable choice wins.

File format and sizing, the parts that get missed

SVG does not work

Most email clients, Outlook included, do not render SVG in message bodies. Use PNG. If someone hands you an SVG logo, export a PNG at the size you need.

Export at double size, display at half

Signature logos look soft on high-density screens when exported at display size. Export at roughly twice the intended width, then set the displayed width in the signature so it renders crisply. Keep the file modest — a signature image is attached to every message you send.

Embedded or hosted

An embedded image travels with the message and always appears, but adds to every email's size. A hosted image keeps messages light but can be blocked until the recipient allows images, which leaves a gap where your logo should be. Embedded is the safer default for a signature.

JPEG is not a workaround

Saving the logo as JPEG does remove the transparency question, but it bakes in a white background permanently and adds compression artefacts around sharp edges and text — exactly the kind of detail a logo is made of. If you want a solid background, use a PNG with that background filled in.

Test it properly before you trust it

Composing a signature and looking at it in the editor tells you almost nothing, because the editor is not the renderer your recipients use. A real test takes five minutes:

  1. Send the signature to yourself.
  2. Open it in Outlook desktop, in Outlook on the web, and on your phone.
  3. Turn dark mode on in each and look again.
  4. Send it to a colleague on Gmail and ask for a screenshot.

If it survives all of those, it will survive your clients. On Windows, signatures live in the Microsoft Signatures folder under your user's AppData — useful if you want to edit the signature HTML directly rather than through the editor.

Still not working? Try this in order

  1. Drag the logo into a browser tab. White there too? The file has no transparency — go to step 5.
  2. Send the signature to yourself and check Outlook desktop, Outlook web and mobile, in both light and dark mode. Note exactly where the box appears.
  3. If it only appears in dark mode, this is the client's fallback. Stop trying to fix the file.
  4. Switch to a logo that reads on both backgrounds, or give it a deliberate brand-coloured background.
  5. If the file genuinely has no transparency, cut the background out properly and export as PNG.
  6. Export at about twice the display width and set the width in the signature so it stays sharp.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my logo file broken if Outlook shows a white background?

No — the file is almost always fine. Outlook adds the white fill during rendering, especially in Dark Mode. Viewing the same PNG in a browser confirms it's still transparent.

Why does my signature look fine while composing but show a white box after sending?

The composing view and the recipient's client render the image differently. Outlook's Dark Mode handling and email client variations can introduce the white background only after the message is sent.

Does saving my logo as JPEG cause the white background?

Yes. JPEG doesn't support transparency, so exporting or converting to JPEG bakes in a solid white background permanently. Always use a PNG for signature logos.

How do I make a transparent logo for my Outlook signature?

Remove the background from your logo and export it as a transparent PNG, then insert it into your signature — ideally inside an HTML table cell so Outlook preserves the transparency.

Can I stop Outlook adding a white box in dark mode?

Not from your side. It is the client's way of keeping a dark logo legible on a dark background, and there is no sender-side setting for it. The practical fix is a logo that works on both light and dark.

Should I use SVG for my email signature logo?

No. Most email clients, including Outlook, do not render SVG in the message body. Export a PNG at roughly twice the display width instead.

Is it better to embed the logo or link to it?

Embedding is safer for a signature: it always appears. A hosted image keeps messages smaller but can be blocked until the recipient allows images, leaving a blank where your logo should be.
